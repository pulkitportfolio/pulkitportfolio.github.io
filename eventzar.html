01 · About

What is EventsZAR?

Think about everything that surrounds one event. Finding out it exists. Deciding to go. Buying the ticket. Talking about it with friends. Buying the team jersey. And on the other side, someone organising all of it and hoping to make money from it.

Today each of those jobs lives in a different app. EventsZAR puts them in one place: a social feed to discover events, event timelines to talk about them, ticketing with QR entry, a merchandise store, chat, polls, and for businesses a complete toolkit to host events, run advertisements and sell.

I designed the entire product across four platforms: the mobile app, the web app, the admin panel that runs the platform and the business management portal that runs its commerce.

02 · The ask

What the project demanded, and what I did about it

This was not a one feature app. It asked for a social network, an e-commerce store, a ticketing system and an advertising platform living together without confusing anyone. Here is how each demand translated into design work.

The project demanded

Serve two very different users in one app

A person attending an event and a business hosting it need completely different things from the same screens.

What I did

Studied both audiences separately, wrote down what each needs from every feature, and drew a clear permission line between normal users and business users that runs through all four platforms.

The project demanded

Complex features that feel instantly familiar

Feeds, checkout, ticket booking, ad campaigns. Each is a whole product category of its own.

What I did

Researched how users already behave on social media, e-commerce and ticket booking apps, and reused the patterns they already know instead of inventing new ones.

The project demanded

Monetisation that does not feel like spam

The business runs on subscriptions, advertisements, ticket and merchandise sales. Users hate feeling sold to.

What I did

Designed ads as native, clearly marked feed cards, gave businesses their own explore tab instead of forcing them into personal space, and put commerce one tap away rather than in the way.

The project demanded

Operations at platform scale

Someone has to moderate posts, verify businesses, settle transactions and manage regions.

What I did

Designed a 20 module admin console on one repeating pattern of KPI cards, filterable tables and preview drawers, with role based access for large teams.

03 · Research

Meet users where they already are

Before drawing a single screen, I spent time understanding how people actually behave inside the three app categories EventsZAR combines. How they scroll a feed, how they trust a checkout, how they hold a ticket at a venue gate. The goal was simple: a first time user should never feel like they are learning a new app.

From social media

Feed, likes, comments, timelines

People scroll, react and share without thinking. → The home feed, event timelines and post interactions follow the exact grammar of social apps, so joining an event conversation feels like second nature.

From e-commerce

Cart, wishlist, tracking, returns

Buyers trust a checkout that behaves like the stores they know. → The merchandise store keeps the familiar cart, coupons, order tracking, returns and refunds journey, nothing clever, nothing surprising.

From ticket booking

Categories, seats, QR entry

Ticket buyers fear two things: wrong ticket and gate trouble. → Clear ticket types, availability states, a wallet of booked tickets in the bottom navigation and QR scanning at entry.

“

Borrowed familiarity is a feature. When one product combines three app categories, the kindest thing a designer can do is let each part behave the way users already expect it to.

04 · Business users

Understanding the people who pay

Business users fund the platform, so I could not design for them from guesswork. I worked through their world scenario by scenario: who they are, what a good week looks like for them, and what they would need from the app at each step. Each scenario was walked through the product end to end, and the flows were reworked over multiple attempts until they held up.

1
Scenario

The event organiser

Runs a public tournament. Needs to create the event, sell tiered tickets, keep the event timeline alive and scan tickets at the gate.

Create event · Ticket types · Timeline · QR scanning
2
Scenario

The brand

Sells merchandise around events and wants reach. Needs a storefront, event exclusive products and ads targeted by area, radius or a specific event.

Storefront · Exclusive merchandise · Ad builder · Targeting
3
Scenario

The community business

Hosts private events for members. Posts privately by adding selected members, or by creating member categories and posting to a category. Runs polls and surveys to hear from the audience.

Private posts · Member categories · Polls and surveys

These scenarios shaped real decisions. The advertisement builder became a guided step by step flow with creative sizes, live previews and three targeting modes, because an organiser is not a media buyer. The business profile became a storefront with followers and a portfolio, because reach matters to them as much as sales. And the whole toolkit sits behind tiered subscriptions, so casual users are never charged for tools they do not need.

05 · Iteration

Explored, questioned, redrawn

None of the key screens survived in their first form. For every important surface I explored more than one direction, questioned what a first time user would actually notice, and redrew until the screen read effortlessly.

The home feed is the clearest example. The design file still carries both the original and the updated feed side by side. The rework tightened the visual hierarchy, made event cards scannable at a glance, and kept the create action reachable with a thumb.

The commerce flows went through the same treatment. Ticket purchase and QR scanning exist in multiple refined variants, and the merchandise and cart screens were simplified until price, quantity and the next step are the only things asking for attention.

The goal of every pass was the same: a product this large has no room for screens that need explaining. Visual polish and ease of use were treated as one job, not two.

06 · Core concept

Three feeds, one clear mental model

All of that research landed in one structural decision: a single home with three switchable feeds. It separates personal life, public discovery and commerce without hiding any of them. Booked tickets, shop and cart stay one tap away in the bottom navigation.

Feed 01

Private

A personal space for posts and events shared with your own circle, organised into member categories like Family or Goa Trip. Business users use the same mechanic to post privately to selected members or a whole category.

Feed 02

Public

The discovery feed. Community events and posts with category filters. Business advertisements appear here as native, clearly marked cards.

Feed 03

Business

An explore tab dedicated to businesses. Browse brands, follow their profiles and see their events, offers and merchandise.

09 · Web app

The same product on a big screen

The web app repeats the mobile mental model: the same three feeds, the same Home, Tickets, Shop and Cart navigation rearranged for desktop. Event timelines open as a side drawer so browsing never loses its place. Nothing to relearn when a user switches devices.

10 · Admin panel

Designed to be run, not just used

A product this size is not finished when users are happy. Someone has to moderate posts, verify businesses, settle money and keep the doors open in every region. So I designed the operations side with the same care as the consumer side: a 20 module console where the platform team can see everything, act on anything and trust the numbers.

Every module repeats one pattern: KPI cards on top, a filterable table below, exports and preview drawers on demand. Learn one module and you have learnt all twenty.

11 · Business portal

Where the business side does business

The fourth platform is a dedicated business management portal, built for the commercial half of EventsZAR. Storefronts, ticket inventory, merchandise, advertisements and their analytics are managed here, business by business, away from day to day platform moderation.

Separating it from the admin panel was a deliberate call: the people who run money and the people who run moderation have different jobs, different risks and different daily screens. Each gets a home built for their work, on the same design system.

12 · Design system

One system behind three products

Everything runs on a shared design system: colour and effect tokens, typography, buttons, badges, dropdowns, loaders and table patterns as reusable component sets with variants. That is what makes 400+ screens across four platforms feel like one product, and what made developer handoff predictable.

13 · Learnings

What this project proves about how I work

I think in products, not screens. Every layout here traces back to a question about people or money: who is this for, what do they already know, and how does the platform earn without annoying anyone.

I do the homework before the pixels. Studying user behaviour across social, commerce and ticketing apps, and walking business scenarios end to end, is what kept a four-products-in-one app from becoming a maze.

I design for the whole lifecycle. Not just the happy path: guest modes, empty states, errors, subscription limits, refunds, moderation and the admin tools that keep it all running.

I build systems, not one-offs. One design system carried 400+ screens across four platforms and kept them consistent for developers.
