01 · The client

Horse-drawn equipment, built since 2003

Alberta Carriage Supply manufactures horse-drawn vehicles in its own shop in Alberta, Canada. Forecarts, working sleds, pleasure carts and people-moving wagons, plus the harness and hardware to drive them, for every horse from Draft down to Mini. Alongside the store they run restoration, wheel repair, custom builds and maintenance services.

Their products are serious purchases. A harness costs hundreds of dollars, a cart runs into thousands, and buyers know exactly what horse they are buying for. The website is where that buying decision happens, and the website was the problem.

02 · The problem

People came for a harness and left at the home page

The existing site had the products and the reputation, but not the flow. Visitors landed, could not tell where to start, and returned from the home page without ever seeing a product. The ones who pushed through faced a buying flow with too many detours to survive.

Finding

Products were hard to find

No clear entry into the catalogue. Category cards duplicated the top navigation, filters were redundant or broken, and there were no breadcrumbs to keep track of where you were.

First impression

The landing felt abrupt

No hero, no statement of what the brand does. A promotional ticker with text cut off at both ends and a cluttered header full of abbreviations did the greeting instead.

Buying

The purchase flow was complex

Inconsistent card CTAs, a cart-only path to checkout and a price slider that did not work. Every extra step gave buyers one more chance to leave, and they took it.

“

The catalogue was never the problem. The path to it was. Traffic was arriving with buying intent and bouncing before the first product. That is a navigation and flow failure, not a demand failure.

03 · The audit

First, prove where it hurts

Before redesigning anything I audited the live site screen by screen: homepage, navigation, category pages, product pages, checkout and mobile. Every finding was annotated on the actual UI and paired with a respectful, actionable recommendation, so the client could see exactly what was costing them buyers and why each change was worth making.

The audit found

Visitors had no starting point

The landing offered a wall of equal choices and no guidance, so people left before choosing.

The redesign answers

A proper hero that states what the brand does, followed by one question: What are you looking for? Four visual categories take buyers straight into the catalogue.

The audit found

Filtering did not match how buyers think

Horse people shop by their horse and their use, not by product taxonomy. The old filters were generic, duplicated and partly broken.

The redesign answers

Categories, subcategories and filters rebuilt around horse size (Mini, Pony, Horse, Draft) and purpose (training, farm, competition, occasions), so narrowing down feels natural.

The audit found

Checkout demanded too much patience

Cart-only checkout, inconsistent CTAs and no feedback along the way meant buyers dropped off mid flow.

The redesign answers

Buy Now goes straight from product to a single-page checkout with saved addresses and cards, a ship or pick-up choice, and a pay button that stays disabled until everything needed is filled.

04 · Navigation

Rebuilt around how horse people actually shop

The old navigation listed what the company makes. The new navigation starts from what the buyer knows: their goal, their horse and what they use it for. Seven entry points lead into the same well-organised catalogue, and a search bar scoped by category catches everyone else. Wherever you land, breadcrumbs and subcategories keep you oriented.

By buyer logic

Shop by Goal

Starter harness, custom wheels, winter sleigh bells. Named after the job to be done, not the SKU.

By buyer logic

Shop by Horse Size

Miniature, Pony, Horse, Draft. The first question any harness buyer answers anyway.

By buyer logic

Shop by Purpose

Pleasure driving, farm work, shows and parades, winter driving, training young horses.

By catalogue

Vehicles & Carts

Two-wheel carts, four-wheel vehicles and specialty builds.

By catalogue

Harness & Horse Gear

Complete sets by size, parts and upgrades, collars, reins and comfort accessories.

By catalogue

Parts & Hardware

Wheels, tires, shafts, poles, brakes, seats, fasteners and finishes.

By confidence

Learning & Guides

A gear quiz, fitting guides and size charts, so first-time buyers can trust their choice before paying.

06 · Design language

Craftsmanship you can feel in the pixels

This is a century-old craft sold by people who build wheels by hand, and the interface needed to carry that. Several serif and sans pairings were explored in the design file before the final choice. The pairing that won gives the brand a voice with heritage and keeps product data crisp at small sizes. Warm where the brand speaks, disciplined where the buying happens.

09 · Impact

What changed after the redesign

The redesign shipped as the client's new storefront, and the difference showed where it matters for a store this specialised.

↑

More visitors stay and shop

The bounce-from-home problem faded. With a clear starting point, people who arrive now actually reach the catalogue.

↑

Sales went up

More of the traffic that was always arriving now completes the journey, and the client saw it in their sales after relaunch.

↓

Fewer mid-flow exits

The shortened, one-page buying flow removed the steps where buyers used to abandon their carts.

✓

Every product is findable

Categories, subcategories and buyer-logic filters mean the right harness for a Mini or a Draft is a few taps away.

10 · Learnings

What this project proves about how I work

I diagnose before I design. The screen-by-screen audit meant every redesign decision answered a documented problem, and the client could follow the reasoning from finding to fix.

I design for the buyer's head, not the org chart. Shop by goal, horse size and purpose exist because that is how a horse person thinks. Navigation that mirrors the customer beats navigation that mirrors the catalogue.

I treat the flow as the product. Individual screens were never the problem here. Removing detours between landing and payment is what turned visitors into buyers.

I finish the whole job. Web and mobile, every state from empty search to disabled pay buttons, so development had no gaps to improvise around.
