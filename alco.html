01 · The client

Fifty years of steel, on a website that whispered

ALCO Energy Solutions designs and builds custom gas processing, carbon capture and modular fabrication equipment out of Edmonton, Alberta. A privately owned Canadian company, in business for over fifty years, shipping engineered modules to projects around the world. It is heavy, precise, unglamorous work, and they are very good at it.

Their home page did not carry any of that weight. The things a buyer in this industry decides on, that ALCO is proven, certified and can take the whole job, were spread thin across the page instead of stated up front. The credibility was all there. It was just filed away.

02 · The brief

They needed it tomorrow. That was the brief.

The request arrived with a deadline attached to it. One redesigned home page, aligned to their goals, ready to show the next morning. No discovery phase, no workshop, no second round to fall back on. Whatever landed in the client's inbox had to be finished work, not a sketch of finished work.

Goal 01

Lead with reliability

Reliability is what this industry actually buys. The first line on the page had to say it, in their own voice.

Goal 02

Let a buyer search

People arrive at a fabricator's site with a spec in mind. Give them somewhere to type it instead of a menu to hunt through.

Goal 03

Prove it early

Fifty years, Canadian ownership, certifications and safety belong near the top, not on an About page nobody opens.

Goal 04

Look like the plant

Industrial, precise, built from their own red and their own photography. A serious buyer trusts a page that looks like the work.

“

Urgency does not remove the process. It removes the padding. Every step below still happened. They were just each given minutes instead of days, and nothing was allowed to be revisited twice.

03 · The clock

Six hours, spent on purpose

Speed is a planning problem before it is a drawing problem. The first thing I did was decide how the six hours would be spent, and then hold to it. Here is where the time actually went.

0:00 to 0:45Research

Read the brief, read the business

Went through the live site and their capability language until I could say what ALCO sells in one sentence. Anything I could not justify from their own material was out of scope for the night.

0:45 to 1:30Moodboard

Moodboard and direction

Pulled references from industrial and engineering brands, locked the palette to ALCO's own red and steel, and picked the typographic voice. Forty five minutes, one board, no second board.

1:30 to 2:15Structure

Structure before pixels

Blocked the page out as one sequence: claim, search, who we are, certifications, safety, solutions, environment, work, careers, contact. Getting the order right first is what made the visual pass fast, because nothing had to move later.

2:15 to 4:30Design 01

Design 01, built to finish

The first full home page, designed at delivery quality rather than concept quality. Real copy, real hierarchy, components made once and reused down the page.

4:30 to 5:30Design 02

Design 02, the alternative

A second direction off the same structure and the same components, so the client had a real choice to react to instead of a single take it or leave it page.

5:30 to 6:00Handover

Prototype and hand over

Wired both directions into scrollable prototypes so the client could open a link on their phone and experience the page, rather than judge a flat image in a deck.

04 · Ideation

The fastest decision is the one you do not make twice

Working this quickly, most of the design happens in what you agree to leave out. Before drawing anything I wrote two lists: what earns its place on the page, and what I was consciously giving up. Once those were set, I did not reopen them.

Kept · earns its place
+
One claim above the fold. “Processing power you can rely on”, with a single supporting line under it.
+
A search field in the hero. The fastest route from a buyer's question to ALCO's answer.
+
Proof close to the claim. Fifty years, Canadian ownership and the certification wall, high on the page.
+
Their own red, used sparingly. One statement band, the buttons, and nothing else.
+
Two ways to act, always visible. Request a Quote for the ready, Contact Us for the curious.
Cut · deliberately not done
−
Inner pages. The brief was the home page. Everything else was noted and left alone.
−
New photography and 3D. The page is composed entirely from imagery ALCO already owns.
−
A full design system. Components were built for this page, tidy enough to extend later.
−
Copywriting from scratch. Their language, tightened. Rewriting a technical business at 2am is how you get it wrong.
−
Micro interaction polish. Motion was described in the handover instead of prototyped frame by frame.
05 · Direction

Forty five minutes of moodboard, then no more debating

The moodboard existed to end arguments, not to start them. I pulled from industrial fabrication, engineering documentation and heavy equipment brands, then reduced it to a palette, a type voice and five words. Every screen after that was checked against those five words instead of against my taste at 3am.

06 · Design 01

Direction one, the page that explains

Light, structured and complete. A slab serif headline states the promise over the shop floor, then a red statement band delivers the one sentence version of the business, and the page walks a buyer through everything they would otherwise have to email and ask for. Who ALCO is, the certifications, the safety record, the four solution areas, the environmental commitment, the work, the careers page and a contact form, in that order.

The claim, in their voice

“Processing power you can rely on.” Reliability is what this industry buys, so the headline says it before anything else does.

A search field in the hero

Technical buyers arrive looking for a spec, not a story. The first input on the page lets them ask for one.

Proof before persuasion

Fifty years, privately owned Canadian, global presence and the certification wall all land in the first two scrolls.

07 · Design 02

Direction two, the page that impresses

Same headline, same search field, same skeleton, opposite mood. Direction two goes dark and cinematic: a full bleed shop floor video carries the first screen, the headline is set in heavy sans with “you can” knocked out to outline, and a single scroll cue replaces the button pair. Below it the page is condensed to the sections that do the selling, so it reads as a statement rather than a brochure.

Atmosphere first

The work fills the screen before a word is read. For a buyer who trusts what a plant looks like more than what a page claims.

Condensed, not cut

The same argument in fewer sections, for a client who would rather the home page set a tone and let inner pages carry detail.

One skeleton, two skins

Both directions share structure and components. That is the only reason a second complete page existed by morning.

The point

Two finished directions beat one perfect one when the client has to decide in the morning. Light and complete, or dark and cinematic, is a question they can answer in a minute. “What do you think of this page” is not.

08 · How I work

What a six hour deadline actually proves

I budget the time before I open the file. Deciding up front that research gets forty five minutes and the moodboard gets forty five is what protects the three hours the design needs.

I keep the process, I shrink it. Research, moodboard, structure, design, prototype. Every stage still happened. None of them were allowed a second pass.

I build components on the first screen, not the fifth. That single habit is the reason a second full direction fit inside the last hour.

I decide what I am not doing, out loud. Naming the cuts early turns a panicked night into a scoped one, and gives the client an honest account of what they are getting.

I hand over something you can open, not just look at. A prototype link answers questions a flat image invites.
